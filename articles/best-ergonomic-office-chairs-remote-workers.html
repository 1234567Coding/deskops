You've already decided to buy a chair. Good — that's the single highest-leverage purchase in a home office. What you need now is a verdict, not another 40-tab research spiral. We sat in six chairs for a minimum of 40 working hours each across three testers (5'4", 5'10", and 6'3"), ran every adjustment through its full range, and scored each chair on the only metric that matters: how your back feels at 5pm on a Friday.

How We Tested

Each chair went through at least one full workweek: video calls, deep-work blocks, the post-lunch slump, all of it. Our testers covered petite (5'4", 130 lbs), average (5'10", 175 lbs), and tall (6'3", 230 lbs) body types. We checked every claim on the box: seat-depth range measured with a tape, lumbar height range, armrest travel in all four dimensions, and whether the tilt mechanism actually keeps your feet planted when you recline. Chairs that couldn't survive a week without something loosening, migrating, or squeaking lost points fast.

Best Overall: Branch Ergonomic Chair ($359)

The Branch hits the exact point of diminishing returns: everything past $360 buys you prestige, not comfort. The seat-depth slider actually slides (2.5" of travel — enough for our 5'4" tester to get proper knee clearance), the lumbar adjusts in both height and tension, and the synchro-tilt keeps feet flat through a full recline. After 40+ hours, our average-build tester's end-of-day back tightness dropped noticeably versus their old $200 chair. The 7-year warranty is the best in this price class. Who it's for: the default answer for anyone 5'2"–6'1" who wants to buy once and stop thinking about chairs. Want the full premium-tier math? Best Budget: HON Ignition 2.0 ($289)

At $289, the Ignition 2.0 is the rare budget chair that isn't secretly a disposable chair. The 4D armrests (a $40 upgrade — take it) and adjustable lumbar cover the adjustments that matter, and the 300-lb capacity with a limited lifetime warranty signals HON expects it to last. The seat foam is firmer than the Branch and the tilt is a simpler mechanism, but after a week our testers rated it 90% as comfortable. Who it's for: anyone spending their own money, or stretching a stipend across a full setup. If the budget pick is 90% as good for 80% of the price, say so — this is that chair.

Best for Back Pain: Steelcase Leap V2, refurbished (~$450)

The Leap V2's LiveBack system — a flexible backrest that changes shape as you move — is the closest thing in chairland to a physical therapist's recommendation. If you already have lower-back pain, this is the chair occupational therapists name most. New ones run $1,000+, which is absurd; the refurbished market (Crandall, BTOD, local office liquidators) sells graded units at $400–500 with new fabric and a 2–5 year seller warranty. Inspect for: smooth cylinder action, intact armrest pads, and no cracked plastic on the back frame. Who it's for: anyone currently in pain, or anyone who wants enterprise-grade ergonomics at a mid-tier price.

Best for Tall People: Herman Miller Aeron, Size C (~$600 used)

Tall remote workers get punished by standard chairs: seat pans too shallow, backrests that end mid-shoulder-blade, headrests that miss the head entirely. The Aeron Size C (the large frame) was designed around bodies up to 6'6", with a higher backrest and deeper seat. The mesh keeps you cool through summer afternoons, and the PostureFit SL lumbar supports the sacrum properly. Buy used — new Aerons at $1,500+ are a luxury tax, and the used market is deep. Who it's for: 6'0"+ sitters, or anyone over 220 lbs who needs the 350-lb-rated C frame.

Best Splurge: Steelcase Gesture ($1,100+)

The Gesture's party trick is the armrest: it moves like a human shoulder, staying under your forearms as you reach for a phone, tablet, or second keyboard position. If you work across multiple devices all day, it's genuinely the most comfortable chair we tested. But comfort-per-dollar peaks hard at the Branch — the Gesture is maybe 8% more comfortable for 3x the price. Who it's for: corporate stipends with no cap, or multi-device power users who've tried everything else. Everyone else: this is the point of diminishing returns, clearly marked.

What to Look For in an Ergonomic Chair

  • Seat depth adjustment: The front edge should clear the back of your knees by 2–3 finger widths. Fixed-depth seats fit only the middle of the bell curve.
  • Synchro-tilt: The seat and backrest recline at a ratio (usually 2:1), so your feet stay planted and your hip angle opens. Knee-tilt and center-tilt are cheaper mechanisms that lift your feet — avoid them.
  • 4D armrests: Height, width, depth, and pivot. If your shoulders creep up toward your ears by 3pm, your armrests are too high or too far apart.
  • Adjustable lumbar: Height and tension. A fixed lumbar bump hits the wrong vertebrae on half the population.
  • Weight capacity with margin: Buy 50+ lbs above your weight. Capacity is a proxy for frame and cylinder quality.

Chairs to Avoid (and Why)

  • $150 "racing-style" gaming chairs: Bucket seats force your shoulders forward, armrests are decorative, and the "lumbar pillow" is a strap-on cushion that migrates by Thursday. Built for 2-hour sessions, not 8-hour workdays.
  • Any chair with fixed armrests: If you can't lower them below desk height, you'll shrug all day or sit too far from the keyboard. Non-negotiable.
  • Mesh-back chairs under $200: Cheap mesh stretches within a year and the lumbar zone goes slack exactly where you need support. Mesh is great at $350+; below that, foam wins.
  • Kneeling chairs and balance balls as primary seating: Fine as a 30-minute rotation option. As an all-day chair, they trade one set of problems for another and there's no backrest for calls.

FAQ

How long should an ergonomic chair last?
A quality chair (Steelcase, Herman Miller, Branch, HON) lasts 7–12 years with normal use — that's why the warranties run that long. A $150 chair lasts 18–36 months before the foam bottoms out. Cost-per-year favors the good chair by year three.
Is a used Herman Miller Aeron worth it?
Yes, with inspection. Check the mesh for tears, the tilt for smooth action, and the cylinder for sinking. Buy from office liquidators or reputable refurbishers rather than random marketplace listings — graded refurbs ($550–650) include new arm pads and a warranty.
Do I really need 4D armrests?
If you type more than 4 hours a day, yes. Armrest height and width are the two adjustments that fix shoulder tension, and you can't get them right with fixed arms unless the chair was molded for your exact body.
What about standing — should I skip the chair?
No. Standing all day causes its own injuries (plantar fasciitis, varicose veins, lower-back fatigue). The research-backed pattern is alternation: a good chair plus a sit-stand routine. See our $1,500 setup guide for the ratio that works.
